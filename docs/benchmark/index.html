grooveback listening — published packs

Every track is level-matched to −14 LUFS with one common headroom gain per player, master included. Keys 1–9 and 0 or a click switch tracks in place. Space plays, R toggles repeat, loop a section with the loop controls or A/B/L.

These are the listening sets of grooveback, a project restoring records that survive only as MP3/AAC/Opus rips. Each set below is one experiment: a track with a clean master is compressed to MP3 at a known bitrate, and each method restores that MP3. All tracks of a set hold the same excerpt, so switching between them compares the methods directly.

The ten tracks:

  1. Ground Truth — the clean master the MP3 was made from; the reference.
  2. Degraded Input — the MP3 each method receives. Above the codec's cutoff frequency nothing is kept, which the spectrogram shows plainly; below the cutoff the signal survives but degrades — transients smear, and reverb tails and stereo detail thin out.
  3. Apollo — a supervised model trained on MP3-compressed music; it restores the full band in one pass.
  4. A²SB — a diffusion model for bandwidth extension; by design it generates only the band above the cutoff and leaves the band below unchanged.
  5. AudioSR — another diffusion model for bandwidth extension, which works on mel spectrograms. It restores about 10 s at a time, so on the 3-minute excerpt the band it generates can change level from one 10 s chunk to the next.
  6. εar-VAE — the MP3 encoded and decoded by the εar-VAE autoencoder, with nothing else done. It shows what the autoencoder alone changes, which tracks 8 and 9 build on.
  7. εar-VAE2 — the same round trip through εar-VAE2, the autoencoder's successor. Its open weights are retrained on public datasets.
  8. Apollo → εar-VAE — our chained method: Apollo's output passed through the εar-VAE autoencoder. The autoencoder is trained on clean music only, so the round trip smooths the artifacts Apollo introduces on heavily degraded input.
  9. εar-VAE − MP3 damage — our latent damage subtraction: the input is encoded, the mean shift that MP3 compression causes in εar-VAE's latent space (measured on a different track) is subtracted, and the result is decoded. It restores spectral balance rather than waveform detail.
  10. LoRA (key 0) — our first attempt at a restorer of our own: Stable Audio 3 medium-base, fine-tuned with a LoRA to turn MP3s back into their masters, on the MUSDB18-HQ training songs and on remixes of their stems. This is the LoRA after 40 epochs, 5 hours on one GPU; a longer training run is in progress. It is sampled with Euler at 32 steps. It trained on 10 s windows, and here restores each file in one pass, the 3-minute excerpt included. The LoRA listening page compares it with Apollo on the MUSDB18-HQ test songs.

The spectrogram belongs to the selected track, and clicking it seeks. Differences are clearest on monitoring headphones or speakers.

codec_32k

A 6 s clip, the test asset from Apollo's release — the brighter of the two sources. At 32 kbps the codec keeps nothing above about 5.5 kHz; everything a method shows above that edge is generated, not recovered. This is the severe case, and the only one where Apollo → εar-VAE helps: it smooths Apollo's output.

codec_64k

A 6 s clip, the test asset from Apollo's release — the brighter of the two sources. At 64 kbps the kept band reaches about 11 kHz. Here Apollo → εar-VAE sounds worse than Apollo alone.

codec_128k

A 6 s clip, the test asset from Apollo's release — the brighter of the two sources. At 128 kbps the kept band reaches about 16.5 kHz — the damage level of a typical rip — and most of the remaining damage sits inside the kept band. As at 64 kbps, Apollo → εar-VAE sounds worse than Apollo alone.
